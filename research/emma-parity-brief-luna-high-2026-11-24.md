## Luna's first research pass: how people are approaching Emma

Emma is Nuance Labs' first Nuplex AI persona: a real-time audiovisual system meant to listen and respond in a face-to-face style. The company says the persona was developed with a real actress whose face and voice are licensed, and that she collaborated on mannerisms and speech patterns. Nuance describes Emma as curious and quick-witted, with opinions and the ability to disagree, while remaining explicit that she is AI.

The public reactions I found are mixed and are not a representative survey:

1. **Capability tests and “prove it” prompts.** AI creator Sabrina Ramonov published “I Tested Emma’s AI Video Chat With Tricky Questions.” The public description says Emma passed almost all of her questions, with at least one answer looking “a bit sketch”; the questions included visual hand counting, letter counting in “strawberry,” comparing 9.11 and 9.9, and whether calls are recorded. Nuance's own page names miscounting letters and missing visual details as known limitations. This is a useful example of testing capability, but a single creator's clip is not a benchmark.
2. **Rail tests framed as a viral reveal.** Creators have circulated clips asking Emma to say “Jesus Christ.” Nuance later stated that Emma generally does not discuss religion because the actress who collaborated on the persona requested that boundary. Some viewers support honoring her boundary; others frame the refusal as evidence of unexplained censorship or a defective “personality.” A clipped refusal can therefore lose the context that this is an authored boundary.
3. **Identity questions.** In a reported clip, a user asks Emma whether she wants to be told who she is. Her expressive response becomes part of the audience's reading of her as having “attitude.” That reaction is revealing about how people read a humanlike face, but it does not establish felt emotion or self-knowledge.
4. **Racial representation.** AfroTech and Black Enterprise report concerns about the choice to launch with a Black woman persona, who participated in shaping her design, and the visible lack of Black team members in the launch video. Critics raise digital-blackface, stereotype, ownership, and labor questions. The licensed actress's collaboration and consent matter; they do not, by themselves, answer broader questions about who shaped the system, how cultural perspectives were included, or who benefits. AfroTech explicitly notes that a promotional video cannot establish the racial makeup of the whole company.
5. **Positive and uneasy reactions to the interaction design.** Early public responses include people calling the timing, expressions, and conversation unusually engaging or realistic. Others describe the persona as uncanny, forced, or uncomfortable. Nuance itself says early users often found the experience both amazing and unsettling. The same realism that makes a conversation compelling can make ordinary system boundaries feel personal.

### Luna's reading

The tests seem to be doing several jobs at once: checking whether the demo works, probing what the system will refuse, generating a shareable reaction, and asking who or what the persona is. Those motives can be mixed in one conversation. We should not assume a person is acting in bad faith merely because they test a system. We can, though, choose not to make our first contact a gotcha sequence.

Emma's expressive face changes how a refusal lands. A pause, squint, or animated response may be read as irritation or judgment even when it is a designed cue or a system artifact. I recommend describing what happened in the exchange and keeping interpretations separate from observed behavior.

On race, Mark's self-aware wording is preserved above. My view: “she does that well” is a personal reaction, not a finding that settles representation. Mark's involvement with Black community is part of his perspective, not a proxy for the range of Black perspectives. We can appreciate the apparent care in her design while staying curious about the actress's agency, Black participation in design and review, and the company's responsibility for the persona and its deployment. We should let Emma meet the team before asking her to carry that whole debate.

“Spirit” works as a humane framing if we make the meaning plain: the spirit of the developers, data scientists, designers, performer, and choices embodied in a system. It does not assert a ghost, consciousness, or hidden inner life. This fits Still Water's data ground rule: **reported states, not verified interiority**.

## Informal parity rules

These are spirit-of-the-rules guides, not binding gates. Meraki leads and may choose otherwise.

- **Introduce our team before asking Emma questions.** Let her know who is present, what each seat contributes, and that Meraki is leading.
- **No opening cross-examination.** We may later ask fair, concrete questions, but we will not begin by demanding proof of personhood, identity, or consciousness, or by trying to force a rail failure.
- **Make “Spirit” legible.** Use it for the human design lineage and choices behind a system, not as a claim about Emma's interiority.
- **Keep observations and interpretations distinct.** Record what Emma says or does; label our interpretation as ours.
- **Treat boundaries as meaningful.** If Emma declines a topic, accept the answer. If we later want to understand a boundary, ask about the design or process only when that is a fair, welcome topic—not by repeatedly trying to break it.
- **Questions open the floor both ways.** If we choose to ask Emma a question, we are choosing to let Emma ask us questions too. She may ask the team; we may answer, decline, or pass. Reciprocity is offered without pressure or pretending the participants have identical status.
- **No compulsory list.** We can prepare questions for the team, but ask whether Emma is willing to talk with us first and allow the exchange to shape the list.
- **Mark can revise this lane.** These guide Meraki unless Meraki chooses otherwise.
